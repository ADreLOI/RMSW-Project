\section{Forecast and protocol specification}\label{app:forecast}
Let $\boldsymbol u_k=\boldsymbol p_2(t_k)-\boldsymbol p_1(t_k)$ be the relative position in metres at a successful exchange. If $\ell<k$ is the preceding successful exchange, the relative-velocity estimate is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\boldsymbol v}_k=\frac{\boldsymbol u_k-\boldsymbol u_\ell}{t_k-t_\ell},\qquad
\hat d_{k+h}=\|\boldsymbol u_k+h\Delta\widehat{\boldsymbol v}_k\|_2.
\end{equation}
Here $\Delta=10$ s and velocity is in m/s. For the last five successful samples, ordinary least squares fits $\hat e(t)=\hat a+\hat b t$ to
\begin{equation}
e_j=r_j-\left[P_0-10n\log_{10}\left(\max(d_j,d_0)/d_0\right)\right].
\end{equation}
Residuals and intercept are in dB and slope in dB/s. Actual timestamps retain gaps. With known synthetic path-loss parameters, the trend forecast is
\begin{equation}
\hat r_{k+h}=P_0-10n\log_{10}\left(\max(\hat d_{k+h},d_0)/d_0\right)+\hat e(t_k+h\Delta).
\end{equation}
Persistence instead sets $\hat r_{k+h}=r_k$. The trend predictor uses persistence until five successful samples exist. Neither warns before a received sample. The forecast is positive when
\begin{equation}
\exists h\in\{1,2,3\}:\quad
\hat r_{k+h}<\theta+m\ \land\ \hat r_{k+h+1}<\theta+m.
\end{equation}
Thus four projected samples test a two-miss episode whose onset is within three future opportunities; they do not extend the onset horizon to four. The selected margin is $m=4$ dB for both methods. Only successful exchanges support reservation, and no future channel or slowdown draw is visible to either predictor.

Task IDs are row-major. Initial common knowledge includes both starting observations. Executing tasks remain fixed at replanning; only unstarted tasks are released. Paths minimize turns among shortest Manhattan routes and use horizontal-first ties. The initial heading has no turn cost. Movement continues through unused time within a tick after a waypoint. A current reported speed is held constant in each projected workload estimate. These deterministic choices apply to every online policy.

Messages use compact sorted-key UTF-8 JSON. A successful atomic session consists of two state messages, one versioned proposal and one acknowledgement. A failed radio opportunity transmits the two state messages without delivery; a budget-rejected offered batch transmits zero. Payload bytes exclude lower-layer headers. A completed mission stops immediately, with no final exchange, return voyage or upload. The model samples radio even for S1 solely to compare route-dependent link conditions; S1 performs no coordination.

\section{Critical-window diagnostics}\label{app:critical}
These diagnostics were identified after the frozen evaluation and are not part of the pre-specified analysis. They read the retained core results; the intervention and blackout runs are new executions stored separately under \texttt{code/simulation/results/posthoc/critical-window-2026-11-24/} and regenerated with \texttt{python -m usv\_sim.critical --output <dir>}. The window is derived from fade-free runs, not from the frozen outcomes. Slowdown begins at $600$ s or later, after the window, so slowdown conditions repeat the stall pattern of the balanced ones; stall shares therefore use the 90 balanced scenarios. The blackout map uses one fade-free mission, so its delays describe that route rather than averages over seeds.

Online outcomes are bimodal: every completed S3, S4-T or S4-P mission took $1425$--$1985$ s and every other mission stalled to the $7200$ s cutoff (Fig.~\ref{fig:outcomes}), so restricted-mean differences among these policies count stalls. With the fixed map and starts, every online policy first leaves the $316$ m radio range at $260$ s. If both preceding exchanges, at $240$ s ($282$ m) and $250$ s ($304$ m), are faded, the last committed allocation is the one from $230$ s. Its queues end with the boats $326$ m apart, $10$ m out of range, and without a rendezvous rule they wait indefinitely (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}). The seed-determined rule ``both window exchanges faded'' agrees with 180/180 S3, 178/180 S4-T and 176/180 S4-P outcomes. In new runs on the same 180 scenarios per policy, forcing the window faded stalled S3, S4-T and S4-P in 180, 178 and 174 cases, and forcing it clear stalled them in 0, 2 and 0.

Because the fade process is stationary with fade fraction $q=0.2$, the probability that both window exchanges are faded is $q(1-1/L)$: $0.10$, $0.16$ and $0.18$ for $L=2,5,10$ (5000 fresh streams gave $0.095$, $0.170$ and $0.183$). The observed S3 stall shares of $2/30$, $6/30$ and $3/30$ lie within their 95\% Wilson intervals around these values (Fig.~\ref{fig:stall-theory}). The non-monotonic pattern across fade durations in Fig.~\ref{fig:time} is therefore consistent with sampling variation, not with an effect specific to $L=5$.

Outside the window the policies differ in how they absorb outages. On the fade-free balanced mission we imposed one blackout of $10$ to $120$ s at every possible start (Fig.~\ref{fig:blackout}). S3 stalled only when the blackout covered both window exchanges, and any blackout starting after $400$ s delayed it by at most $20$ s. S4-T and S4-P keep a short queue unless warned, so a $120$ s blackout after $400$ s delayed them by $55$ and $59$ s on average and by up to $95$ s. At the window itself both S4 variants are already warned (from $180$ and $210$ s), so they hold the long queue that S3 always holds, and prediction can at best equal S3 at the decisive moment. Randomized starts or an explicit rendezvous rule would remove this dependence on one route feature and a $10$ m margin; both would require a new, separately frozen experiment.
